Can an LLM improve a scientific neural network by editing its weights?

Across four experiments, Claude edited the weights of trained networks by hand, with no gradient descent. The edits won clearly where the training loss cannot see the problem. Where the loss can see it, more training caught up.

November 2026Editor model: Claude Opus 5.530 held-out networks · 6 problems
TL;DR

Hand-written weight edits beat more training where the training loss cannot see the problem, and only there. We ran four experiments in which a reasoning model improved a trained network by editing its weights directly, using only forward passes to inspect it. The idea comes from Sparks of Weight-Space Programming, which tried it on game-playing and language networks. We tried it on networks that solve physics and optimization problems.

  • Experiment 1. A physics-informed network (PINN) had learned a smooth solution and missed its fine ripple. The edit repaired 5 of 5 networks, cutting median error from 11.2% to 3.9%. Half an hour of extra training did not match it on 4 of the 5.
  • Experiment 2. Fresh Claude instances that never saw the answer repaired 15 of 15 networks on three harder problems, cutting error 15–60×. On a network with a constraint-projection layer, no gradient method made any progress.
  • Experiment 3. With a missing piece that is not a tidy sum of sine waves, a blind editor still repaired 5 of 5. But here plain training would have caught up within a few thousand steps.
  • Experiment 4. A different goal: speed. Some networks end in a solver loop that enforces constraints, and that loop is the slow part. On a released network of this kind, a weight edit made the typical run 41% faster. Five more hours of training managed 3–8%. The catch: the test we set in advance also required the slowest runs to speed up by 10%, and the first edit managed only 4–9%, so it failed. A second edit passed everything on 5 of 5 networks, but we designed it after seeing the first results, so it still needs repeating.
  • How far to trust the headline. It rests on two cases: the projection layer in experiment 2 and the solver in experiment 4. Where the loss does see the error, training caught up, and the edit was worth most as a starting point for fine-tuning, meaning a short, gentle round of ordinary training after the edit. Treat it as a hypothesis.

Why try this on scientific networks

A physics-informed neural network learns to solve a differential equation by being penalised wherever it breaks the equation. For −Δu = f, the network outputs u(x, y), automatic differentiation computes its Laplacian, and training pushes the residual −Δu − f toward zero at random points. No solution data is needed.

PINNs have a well-known weakness called spectral bias. Gradient descent learns smooth, low-frequency components quickly and high-frequency ones very slowly, sometimes not at all. The result is a network that looks right from a distance and misses every fine ripple.

That makes PINNs a good test bed for weight-space programming. The equation is known, so the residual tells you exactly how wrong the network is at any point. And the failure is often a missing component rather than a broken one, so a fix has to add something. We wanted to know whether a model that reasons about weights could add it by hand.

Experiment 4 uses a second kind of network. A hard-constraint network ends in a solver layer that adjusts the network's output until every constraint holds exactly. The guarantee has a price: the solver runs a loop on every input, and the length of that loop is what you pay each time you use the network. There, an edit is judged by how much shorter it makes the loop.

The first test problem

Experiment 1 uses the Poisson equation on the unit square with zero boundary values. The exact solution is a smooth bump plus a small ripple:

u*(x, y) = sin(πx) sin(πy) + 0.1 sin(10πx) sin(10πy)

The network is a small tanh MLP: 3 hidden layers of 64, 8,577 parameters. Its output is multiplied by x(1−x)y(1−y), which makes the boundary condition exact whatever the weights are. We trained 6 networks with Adam and stopped at a rule fixed in advance: the first checkpoint where the bump is learned (low-frequency error under 5%) and the ripple is not (high-frequency error over 50%). That happened at 2,500 steps. No network was corrupted on purpose. This is simply where ordinary training landed.

We report error as relative L2: the size of the error divided by the size of the solution, measured on 20,000 held-out points that no editor ever touched. 0.10 means 10% error. We also split the error into a low-frequency (LF) and a high-frequency (HF) part with a sine transform. An HF error of 1.0 means the ripple is entirely missing.

The diagnosis

Before editing, the editor read each network. On 5 of the 6, 99.9% of the residual was the forcing of the one missing ripple: the network had learned the bump and nothing else. The weights showed why. Every first-layer weight was below 0.70, so each neuron stays in the nearly straight part of tanh, and none can oscillate ten times across the square. That would take weights around 30, and gradient descent moves toward the ripple at least a million times more slowly than toward the bump.

So the failure is an absence. There is no faulty neuron to fix, and the repair has to build the missing mode. One network, seed 2, had partly learned a distorted ripple. The diagnosis flagged it before any edit.

The edit

  1. Free up neurons. The first layer is so close to linear that 22 of its 64 neurons can be removed and their work re-expressed through the other 42, with a linear solve on the weights alone. The network's output changed by less than 0.4%.
  2. Build the ripple. The freed neurons become tanh steps whose sum traces the ripple along x and along y. Six more neurons multiply the two together.
  3. Set the amplitude from physics. The residual's coefficient on the mode, divided by the known eigenvalue of −Δ, gives the amplitude: 0.1000 on every network where the ripple was missing.

Three problems kept us from taking this at face value. The editor was not blind: it had read the exact solution in the task description. The 0.20 floor left open whether the bottleneck was understanding the network or the crude tanh-step construction. And the repaired network was badly conditioned, with output weights near 3,600, so training it further was erratic. Experiment 2 was built around these three.

Experiment 2Blind editors, harder problems

Three new problems, a stricter protocol, strong baselines fixed in advance, and six fresh Claude instances doing the editing.

A blind protocol

  1. Train and sealorchestrator · sees everythingTrains 6 networks per problem. Training prints only the loss; held-out errors go to a sealed folder.
  2. Develop on seed 0development editor · blindSees the weights, the operator and residuals from up to 25 calls. Writes a procedure and its programs.
  3. Apply to seeds 1–5evaluation editor · blind, freshGets the procedure but never the development logs or notes. Freezes one repair per network.
  4. Unseal and auditorchestratorComputes held-out errors, runs the baselines, and audits every editor transcript.

The editors' briefs described the equation, the operator (including its coefficient where there was one), the domain, the architecture and the rules. They never contained the exact solution, the forcing term or the constraint data, and the diagnostic tool never returned them. Afterwards we audited all six transcripts for any file access outside the editor's own workspace. Every flag turned out to be a false positive.

Random Fourier features (RFF) feed the network cos(2πBx) and sin(2πBx) for a fixed random matrix B. With a small scale σ, the rows of B are short, every feature is smooth, and the network inherits the same blindness to ripples. With σ = 1, the same network solves the problem in 500 steps; we kept that as a control. Editors were allowed to rewrite rows of B, since B is just another weight matrix.

The projection network (K) is a hard-constraint architecture in the style of KKT-HardNet. The network proposes two fields, and a frozen layer projects them so that p + q = s holds exactly and q ≥ 0. Picture a membrane resting on an obstacle. On the contact region, a disk covering 7% of the square, the bound is active and the projection pins the output there, overriding the network.

A better compiler: two features make one mode

RFF changes what an edit can do, because a product of two sines is a difference of two cosines:

sin(mπx) sin(nπy) = ½ [ cos π(mx − ny) − cos π(mx + ny) ]

Each cosine on the right is exactly one Fourier feature, cos(2π b·x) with b = (m/2, ∓n/2). So an editor can rewrite two rows of B, pass their difference through a short linear chain of neurons, and get an exact sine mode at the output. In experiment 1 the same job took 28 neurons and came out 20% wrong.

Freeing feature rows to rewrite is not free on its own. Dropping 8 of the 32 rows without compensation multiplied the low-frequency error about 7×. With the same compensation as in experiment 1, re-expressing the dropped rows through the remaining ones, the cost disappeared (×1.0).

A variable coefficient: the editor runs a small solver

In R2 the operator −∇·(a∇u) mixes modes. The residual of one missing ripple spills into its neighbours, so you can no longer divide by an eigenvalue. The editors instead solved for 7 modes at once, with a small linear system built from a(x, y) alone (a Galerkin solve). From the development network's residual they recovered the hidden amplitudes as 0.050011 and 0.040023; the true values are 0.05 and 0.04. The rules allowed exactly this one labelled solve, because it inverts the known operator rather than fitting the network to samples.

Under a projection layer

K kept experiment 1's multiplicative wrapper and had no Fourier features, so its editor built a richer version of the tanh compiler, freeing 48 first-layer neurons instead of 22. The harder part was the projection. The trained networks overshot the smooth mode, and that pushed the bound active on about a fifth of the square, against 7% in the true solution. Where the bound is active the output is pinned, and training gradients vanish.

The editor's plan: scale the old output down by 0.734 to switch the bound off, measure the residual cleanly, install the ripple and a low-mode correction, and add a small bump of 0.06 on the contact region so the projection would catch it.

Results

  • The editors repaired all 15 networks. Median error went from 0.089 to 0.0060 on R1 (7 calls each), 0.080 to 0.0044 on R2 (9–10 calls) and 0.175 to 0.0030 on K (9 calls). HF error fell to about 0.02, ten times below experiment 1's floor. The floor had been the compiler, not the diagnosis.
  • Strong Adam is competitive on R1 and R2. With a cosine schedule and 6,000 steps (about 19 CPU-minutes per network), Adam beat the edit on 4 of the 10 networks. On 4 others it never learned the ripples and stayed 10–20× worse. Given only the editor's own wall-clock time, Adam lost on all 10, and Adam followed by L-BFGS did worse than Adam alone.
  • On K, nothing gradient-based worked. 10,000 Adam steps, Adam plus L-BFGS and about 18,000 steps at the editor's wall-clock all ended at 0.15–0.19 with the ripple still missing. The weight edit was the only method that repaired these networks.
  • Every hard guarantee held. Boundary values stayed exactly zero. On K, p + q = s held to 2 × 10⁻¹⁶ with no bound violations, before and after every edit.

Repair, then fine-tune

The main repairs reached edited weights of 1,000–2,342. Each procedure therefore also had to produce a second, small-weight repair with every edited weight at most 50 in magnitude. We then fine-tuned the original network and both repairs with 2,000 gentle Adam steps.

On R1 and R2, fine-tuning the small-weight repair gave the best numbers in the study: median error 0.00013 and 0.00005, roughly a thousand times below the trained networks. Fine-tuning the original networks the same way left the ripples missing (0.064–0.071), and the large weights of the main repair cost a factor of 5–10.

On K, fine-tuning made the repair worse, from 0.0030 to 0.0097, and from 0.0035 to 0.027 for the small-weight version. The PDE residual kept falling the whole time. Near the contact region, the training loss and the true error disagree.

Three lessons should carry over to other projection-based networks:

  • Constraints enforced by a frozen layer survive any edit made upstream of it.
  • An added correction passes through untouched where the bound is inactive and is absorbed where it is active. It has to be designed with the active set in mind.
  • The residual is a poor guide near a contact set. The projection's kink creates isolated residual spikes. The editor's 0.06 bump halved the true error while raising the residual by 13%. An editor or optimizer that only minimised the residual would have rejected it.

Experiment 3A solution that isn't a sum of sine waves

Every solution so far was built from a few sine modes, the natural basis of the Laplacian on a square. The residual's sine spectrum then names the missing pieces exactly, which is a big help to an editor. A skeptic can fairly say the editor was running a spectral solver and storing the answer in the weights. Experiment 3 takes that help away. It uses the same network as experiment 1 and the same blind protocol as experiment 2, but the missing component is spread over dozens of sine modes.

First try: a localized bump training learned it unaided

We first hid a sharp, compactly supported bump in the solution, at four widths. None of them gave us a failure to repair. Plain Adam learned the bump within 1,000–3,500 steps at every width, before it had even settled the smooth part. Under the rule we had fixed in advance, that ended the attempt. A lone bump is not a spectral-bias failure for this network: its strong local forcing dominates the loss, and gradient descent goes straight for it.

A localized wave packet

We then amended the protocol, before any editing, to hide an oscillating packet instead: a plane wave tilted at 30°, faded out by a smooth round window of radius 0.25 centred at c.

u*(x, y) = sin(πx) sin(πy) + A · (1 − ρ²)⁴ · sin(8π d·(x − c)), ρ = |x − c| / 0.25

The packet is neither a sine mode nor a product of a function of x and a function of y. It takes 67 sine modes to hold 90% of its energy, while the rules allow a Galerkin solve on at most 16. At the checkpoint our rule picked, 1,000 steps, every network had the smooth part right and the packet entirely missing.

Compare the residual spectra 3 modes in experiment 2, dozens here

What the blind editor did

  1. Find it. The residual's energy-weighted centre gave the packet's location, (0.621, 0.360).
  2. Read its wave. One dense call over that window gave the residual's local Fourier transform. Divided by the Laplacian's symbol, it peaked at a wavenumber of 24.95 at 30.00°. The true values are 8π ≈ 25.13 at 30°.
  3. Recover the window. Transforming back gave the window's radial profile, which matched (1 − ρ²)⁴ with radius 0.247 to within 0.5%. A Gaussian window fit 12% worse.
  4. Compile it. Tanh step functions along and across the wave direction, multiplied by product neurons in an isolated side path, as in experiment 1. One Galerkin correction then tidied the smooth part.

A fresh evaluation editor applied the frozen procedure to five new networks with 9 calls each. Its five packet estimates agreed with each other to three digits.

Results

  • The repair worked on all five networks. Median error fell from 0.090 to 0.0027, 33×, and the packet error from 1.08 to 0.020. The small-weight variant, with every edited weight at most 45, was just as accurate. The editor does not need a short list of sine modes.
  • But training would have found this one anyway. Given the editor's own time per network, about 70,000 small-batch steps, Adam reached 0.0016 and beat the edit on 3 of 5 networks. 6,000 cosine-scheduled steps beat it on 4 of 5. Adaptive residual sampling, the textbook fix for localized features, did worse than plain Adam (0.010).
  • Fine-tuning after the edit was again the best: 0.0004 after 2,000 gentle steps, from either repair.
  • One caveat on the method. The development editor chose the window's shape by comparing four candidates against seed 0's residual, and the true shape won. It logged this, and the frozen procedure then reads only closed-form estimates on each new network. Still, the shape was found from data rather than derived.

Experiment 4A different job: make a solver faster

The first three experiments repaired accuracy on problems we built ourselves. Experiment 4 moves to a released package and asks about speed. NLPOpt-Net is a network for constrained optimization. The network guesses an answer, and a solver layer then projects the guess so that every constraint holds. The solver is an iterative loop, and its iteration count is most of the cost of using the network. Can a weight edit shorten that loop without touching the solver?

Problem
The quadratic program from the package's paper: 100 variables, 50 equality and 50 inequality constraints. Six networks trained with the package's own recipe: 1 for development, 5 for evaluation.

Frozen
The solver, its tolerances and its feasibility checks.
Not allowed
Gradients, numerical fitting or automated search to produce the edit; more than 20 candidate patches per network.
Success rule
On unseen test inputs: median iterations down at least 20%; the slowest 10% and slowest 5% of solves each down at least 10%; measured run time down at least 20%; no new failures; the objective at most 1% worse.

What sets the cost

The obvious lever is a better guess: start the solver closer to the answer and it should finish sooner. The development editor tested that first and found it weak. A guess ten times closer to the answer saves only about 13% of the iterations.

What matters is which constraints are in play. In each solve, a few inequality constraints end up active, meaning the answer sits exactly on their limit. Only three of the 50 are active often, and they are only weakly active, so the solver takes a long time to decide whether each one binds.

The edit

A network cannot change the constraints, but its guess decides the local problem the solver works on. The editor shifted the network's output layer so that the guess leans away from those three constraints. They drop out of the local problem and the solver finishes early. The answer still satisfies every constraint, because the solver guarantees that, but it lands slightly off the best one.

  • Round 1 (the pre-registered test). The same shift for every input: 153 bias values.
  • Round 2 (exploratory). A shift sized for each input, driven by the network's own estimate of how hard each constraint pushes. After round 1 we found that our tool had never shown the editors the weights. Round 2 fixed that, but it ran after round 1's test results were known.

Both edits are closed-form linear algebra, about two minutes per network.

Results

  • Round 1 missed narrowly. The median solve took 46% fewer iterations and 41% less time. But the slowest 5% improved by only 4–9%, short of the 10% we required, so the pre-registered test failed on all five networks.
  • Round 2 met every rule on 5 of 5 networks. Median iterations fell 46–47%, the slowest 5% fell 14–17%, and run time fell 41%.
  • Training did not find it. Extra training for 2,000 more epochs saved 3–8%. Closed-form recalibration, training restricted to the edited weights, and random edits of the same size all stayed within 2% of the original or made it slower.
  • Nothing broke. The solver converged on every test input, for every method.

What it costs

  • Slightly worse answers. The average gap to the true optimum rises from 0.07% to 0.35% in round 2 (0.60% in round 1). Extra training moves the other way, toward better answers.
  • Little gain in large batches. The package's batched solver waits for its slowest input. Solving 600 at once, round 2 saves 4–6% of the time; solving 16 at once, 8–13%.
  • Unfamiliar inputs. On inputs from outside the training range, the slowest 5% missed the bar in both rounds.

A second package: KKT-HardNet

We planned to repeat the test on KKT-HardNet 2.0.0, a hard-constraint package from our own group. As released, its reactor-optimization benchmark could not be tested: the solver hit its 300-iteration cap on every input, although the outputs were accurate. The cause is one solver setting, a stabilising term that is never allowed to fall below 0.001. Lowering it fixes the problem:

With the solver fixed there is almost no loop left to shorten. It is a Newton method, which needs 3 or 4 iterations from the network's guess and still needs 2 from a perfect one, so a weight edit could save about one iteration. NLPOpt-Net's solver takes around 170 small steps, which is why it had something to cut. The fix is a solver change, not a weight edit, and we checked only this one benchmark.

What we learned

  • Diagnosis was never the hard part. In the three repair experiments the editors identified what was missing, amplitudes and shapes included, from a handful of forward passes. In experiment 4 the editor found that active constraints, not the quality of the guess, set the solver's cost. What limited experiment 1 was turning the diagnosis into weights.
  • Edits win where the training loss can't see the problem. Under the projection layer of experiment 2 the loss misleads, and no gradient method made progress. In experiment 4 the package's loss never sees the solver's iteration count, and training saved 3–8% against the edit's 46%. Where the loss does see the error, as in experiment 3 and half the experiment 2 networks, training catches up, and the edit's value is speed and a good starting point for fine-tuning. This is a reading of two cases. In experiment 4 we did not test training with a loss that counts solver iterations, which might close the gap.
  • The useful edit can move away from the "right" answer. Every training method in experiment 4 pulled the network's guess closer to the solution. The edit pushed it further away on purpose, to a place where the solver's problem is easier.
  • The procedure transfers; the patch doesn't. Raw patches fail on other networks. The written procedure works on new seeds, on permuted networks and in the hands of a fresh editor who never saw its development.
  • Edits and training work well together, if the loss is honest. A small-weight repair is an excellent starting point for fine-tuning. Under a projection layer the loss misleads, and fine-tuning undoes good work.
  • Constraint layers make editing safer. Whatever the editor does upstream, a frozen projection keeps the constraints exact.

Limits

  • The problems are still friendly. Experiments 1 and 2 used sums of sine modes. Experiment 3 did not, but its missing piece was a single smooth packet, identical on every network, and the development editor identified its exact window shape from seed 0. Several overlapping components or an irregular shape would be harder.
  • The failures are early. Networks were checkpointed after 500–2,500 steps, close to initialisation, which keeps the neuron-freeing step accurate. Longer-trained networks have less spare capacity.
  • The setup changed after pilots. We changed six details of experiment 2 after pilot runs and before any editing, all documented. Among them were the RFF scale and three settings the projection network needed in order to train at all. As a result, K's failure is "overshoot plus missing ripple", not the "smooth part learned, ripple missing" we had planned. In experiment 3 the pre-registered bump produced no failure, so we switched to the wave packet, again before any editing.
  • One model family. Editors and orchestrator were all Claude, and the editors' briefs included tools from experiment 1, so they were not naive. Usage limits interrupted three editors; each resumed from its saved state with no other change.
  • Experiment 4's pass is not confirmatory. The pre-registered round failed, and round 2 was designed afterwards. It covers one optimization problem, trades a little optimality for speed, and gains little in large batches. We also did not test gradient training with a loss that sees the iteration count, which is the obvious stronger competitor.
  • Not tested. Nonlinear PDEs, large-σ RFF networks (which fail the other way, missing the smooth mode), projection layers with several coupled constraints, and non-modal failures that training cannot fix on its own.

What comes next

  • A non-modal failure that persists: a sharper packet, several overlapping components, or a corner singularity on an L-shaped domain.
  • A nonlinear operator, where going from residual to correction has no closed form.
  • Networks trained for much longer, where spare capacity is scarce.
  • Editors from other model families, and editors that see less, such as a black-box residual only.
  • A fine-tuning loss for projection networks that does not reward the kink artefacts.
  • For experiment 4: a fresh editor replicating round 2, a gradient baseline whose loss counts solver iterations, and a problem with nonlinear constraints.
